I am the supervisor responsible for printing the production-order job cards for Lines 3 and 5. I provide these job cards to the Sign-Out Control employees, who use the production-order numbers to prepare the pallet placards. They are also required to scan the barcode on each job card using a handheld scanner and verify the information displayed, including the item number, location code, quantity, finished quantity, and unit of measure.

Bilah is the experienced Sign-Out Control employee and speaks English. Zenaida Garcia is still in training. The Spanish-speaking employee who had been training Zenaida is no longer employed by the company. As a result, Bilah was responsible for preparing the paperwork for both lines while also assisting Zenaida.

Instead of separating the job cards needed by the mixer operators from those needed by Sign-Out Control, Bilah left all the job cards together on the tableenade where he normally prepares the paperwork before the start of the shift. During this process, he mistakenly selected the production-order number intended for the mixer operation, which is used to post flour-dough batches. He wrote this number on a placard intended for finished tortilla pallets, which should have had a different production-order number.

Zenaida was expected to scan the barcode directly from the correct tortilla production-order job card, verify the information displayed, and then post the tortilla output. Instead, she relied on the incorrect number written on the placard and manually entered it into the tablet. Consequently, the finished tortilla output was posted against the dough-batch production order.

The error continued for most of the shift and was discovered only after the system showed an unusually high dough output. Tortilla output is measured in dozens, while dough output is measured in batches. The expected maximum was approximately 21 dough batches for the shift, but 8,342 batches were posted.

The driver responsible for transferring the finished tortilla pallets to the main plant also transferred all 8,342 units without verifying whether the quantity represented dozens of tortillas or dough batches. Because the inventory location was changed during the transfer, the item-tracking number also changed, making it impossible to reverse the quantities through the normal process.
